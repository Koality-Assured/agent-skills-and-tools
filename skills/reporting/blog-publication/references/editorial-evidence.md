# Technical blog editorial evidence

This reference records writing practices and research relevant to technical blog posts. Sources were checked on 2026-10-09 (America/Chicago). Style guidance is advice, not a measured promise of traffic or reader retention.

## Practices supported by the sources

- Name the intended reader and what the post should help them understand or decide. Put the useful result early, then explain its scope.
- Use a specific, informative headline and descriptive sentence-case headings. Keep sentences focused, write conversationally without forced informality, and read the draft aloud.
- Give readers a concrete takeaway they can act on. Address readers, use an occasional question, or add an aside only when it helps explain or invite a useful response; do not use these as engagement tricks.
- Treat engagement advice as a hypothesis for the publication's audience. Do not promise a traffic lift without that site's analytics or a controlled experiment.

## Sources and limits

- Google for Developers, [Audience](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience), updated 2025-03-28 UTC; accessed 2026-10-09. Guidance to define audience, goals, and reader familiarity, and avoid assuming the reader knows the writer's context. It is instructional guidance, not an engagement experiment.
- Google for Developers, [Documents](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/documents), updated 2025-07-07 UTC; accessed 2026-10-09. Recommends leading with the useful result and organizing material around reader needs. It does not quantify effects on blog readership.
- Google Developer Documentation Style Guide, [Voice and tone](https://developers.google.com/style/tone), updated 2026-05-27 UTC; accessed 2026-10-09. Recommends clear, direct, conversational language without slang, jargon, or filler. Style guidance, not a traffic study.
- Google Developer Documentation Style Guide, [Headings and titles](https://developers.google.com/style/headings), updated 2026-06-08 UTC; accessed 2026-10-09. Recommends descriptive, logically nested, sentence-case headings. Style guidance, not a traffic study.
- Google for Developers, [Short sentences](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/short-sentences), updated 2025-03-28 UTC; accessed 2026-10-09. Recommends one main idea per sentence and removing filler. Style guidance, not a traffic study.
- Zou and Hyland, [“Think about how fascinating this is: Engagement in academic blogs across disciplines”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeap.2019.100809), *Journal of English for Academic Purposes* (2020); accessed 2026-10-09. Corpus analysis of 132 academic blog posts describes rhetorical devices such as reader address, directives, questions, asides, and sharedness, with differences across disciplines. It did not measure reader behavior or establish that these features cause engagement; academic blogs are not a proxy for technical-industry traffic.
- [“Titles framed as questions reduce reader engagement”](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.70031), *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, first published 2026-07-09; accessed 2026-10-09. Four studies: Reddit posts (N=53,030), academic articles (N=3,078,791), 22,743 online-news headline A/B experiments, and a preregistered lab study (N=400). The studies found lower engagement for question-framed titles in their tested settings. They do not establish the same effect for technical blogs, every audience, or every title; use the result as a reason to prefer informative titles over a question-title formula, not as a universal rule.
- Williams and Rainer, [“How do empirical software engineering researchers assess the credibility of practitioner–generated blog posts?”](https://doi.org/10.1145/3319008.3319013), EASE 2019; accessed 2026-10-09. Online survey of 43 software-engineering researchers. Respondents valued reasoning, clear writing, empirical data, and reporting how data was collected. This is a small survey of researcher opinions, not a general reader study; the authors call for independent replication.
- Shulman, Markowitz, and Rogers, [“Reading dies in complexity: Online news consumers prefer simple writing”](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn2555), *Science Advances*; published online 2024-06-05 (issue date 2024-06-07); accessed 2026-10-09. More than 30,000 field experiments at *The Washington Post* and Upworthy, plus follow-up reader studies. The research found a preference for simpler headlines in its tested news settings. It does not establish the same effect for technical blogs, every audience, or downstream task success; use clear phrasing as a readability practice, not a traffic guarantee.
